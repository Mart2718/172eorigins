Where does e come from?

Math 172 Precalculus

    One dollar, one year, 100% interest

    Imagine a bank with an outrageous offer: deposit $1, and it pays 100% interest per year. After one year, you have your dollar plus a dollar of interest.

    Bank A pays once a year

    $1.00

    Bank B pays 50% twice a year

    Start
    $1.00
    After 6 months
    $1.00 × 1.5 = $1.50
    After 12 months
    $1.50 × 1.5 = $2.25

    Same 100% for the year, but Bank B wins. In the second half, you earn interest on the interest you already earned. That's compounding.

    So what if a bank chopped the year into smaller and smaller pieces: every month, every day, every second?

    Check your thinking

    From daily to every second, n gets about 86,000 times bigger, but you gain only about a third of a cent. Look at the two bars. What two forces are fighting each other?

    More periods push the total up. But each period's rate, 1/n, shrinks at exactly the same pace. The two effects nearly cancel, so the total creeps toward a ceiling instead of exploding. That ceiling is a specific number.

    Teacher note

    Pause at "monthly" and "daily" and ask for a predicted value for the next stop before sliding. Common misconception: "more compounding always means a lot more money." The table's shrinking gaps make the limit concrete before any limit notation appears.

    The ceiling has a name

    e ≈ 2.718281828459045…

    As n grows without bound, (1 + 1/n)n gets as close as you like to one number. Mathematicians call it e, a letter Leonhard Euler used in the 1700s. Like π, its decimals never end or repeat.

    π shows up whenever something is round. e shows up whenever something changes at a rate proportional to how much of it there is: money earning interest on itself, a hot drink cooling faster when it's hotter, a body clearing medicine faster when there's more of it.

    From $1 at 100% to any rate and any time

    With rate r for t years, compounding continuously multiplies what you start with by

    ert

    Why do r and t end up in the exponent?

    (1 + r/n)nt = [ (1 + r/n)n/r ]rt

    Let m = n/r. The inside becomes (1 + 1/m)m, which heads to e as m grows. What's left outside is the exponent rt.

    Growth or decay: one curve, one dial

    Most real models use y = ekt. Move k and watch what the sign does.

    Check your thinking

    At k = 0 the graph is flat at 1. Why does that make sense in a real situation?

    A rate of zero means nothing changes: no interest, no cooling, no clearing. e0 = 1, so the quantity stays exactly what it started as.

    T(t) = Ts + (T0 − Ts)e−kt

    temperature of the surroundings

    Newton's Law of Cooling: the gap between the coffee and the room shrinks exponentially. A big gap means fast cooling; a small gap means slow cooling.

    Where else: food-safety rules for cooling cooked food, engineers sizing heat sinks for phones and laptops, and forensic scientists estimating time since an event all use this same equation.

    A(t) = A0e−kt

    A0
    amount absorbed from one dose (400 mg here)
    k
    elimination rate; the body clears more per hour when there's more to clear
    t
    hours since the dose

    Pharmacists usually describe k by its half-life, the time to clear half: k = ln 2 ÷ half-life.

    Teacher note

    Each scenario is gated behind a prediction so students commit before they see the curve. Discussion move: ask which part of each equation plays the role of "starting amount" and which plays "rate." Savings grows (k > 0); coffee and medicine decay (k < 0). Coffee is the stretch case because the curve levels off at room temperature rather than at zero.
